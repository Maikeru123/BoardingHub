1. Organization Name

BoardingHub

BoardingHub is a proposed online housing-service venture and an implemented web application that centralizes boarding-house discovery, verified property information, room availability, tenant–owner communication, rental requests, payment records, reviews, reports, and administrative moderation. Its operating idea is summarized by the project’s tagline: “Find a Place. Manage with Ease.”

2. Industry Type

Property Rental / Real Estate Technology / Online Housing Services

The organization operates in the rental-housing sector, concentrating on affordable boarding houses, rooms, shared accommodation, bed spaces, and studio units. Unlike a general real-estate marketplace focused on property sales, BoardingHub supports the complete boarding-house journey: discovering a property, checking rooms, contacting an owner, requesting a rental period, recording payments, and reviewing a completed stay.

3. Organization Address

Davao City, Philippines

Davao City is the proposed initial operating base and pilot market because of its concentration of universities, colleges, workplaces, and people seeking short- or medium-term accommodation. The application is not technically limited to Davao City: its address selector stores official ten-digit Philippine Standard Geographic Code (PSGC) values for province, city/municipality, and barangay, allowing future expansion throughout the Philippines.

5. Organization’s Brief History

BoardingHub began as a response to a common problem experienced by students and employees who relocate: boarding-house information is distributed across social-media groups, Marketplace posts, printed advertisements, and personal recommendations. These sources often omit the exact address, current price, room type, amenities, rules, photographs, and actual availability.

The original proposal focused on a searchable listing website for Davao City. The project has since developed into a working, full-stack web application. The current repository includes authenticated role-based dashboards, property and room management, structured Philippine addresses, verification, public search and filters, tenant–owner inquiry threads, rental requests with date-conflict checking, payment records with proof images, completed-rental reviews, listing reports, user feedback, and administrative oversight.

This evolution changes the system from a simple advertising directory into a controlled rental-management platform. The current design preserves the organization’s original goal—making boarding-house information easier to find—while adding safeguards and workflows required to keep listings and transactions useful over time.

7. Business Process

The processes below use the template’s object-and-action method. Objects identify people, records, or documents; actions identify what participants and the system do.

7.1 Account Registration and Profile Management

A new user registers as either a tenant or property owner. The authentication service validates the credentials and creates the account. A database trigger creates the matching profile using only the tenant or owner role; public registration cannot assign administrator privileges. The user confirms the email, signs in, and may update personal and contact information. Administrators can suspend an account when necessary.

Objects: User, authentication account, profile, role, account status.

Actions: Register, validate, confirm, authenticate, create profile, update, suspend, sign out, reset password.

7.2 Boarding-House Setup and Verification

An owner creates a boarding-house record and enters its name, description, contact number, rules, payment terms, amenities, and PSGC-based address. The owner uploads property photos and selects one primary image. Before submission, the system requires a complete structured address and a primary photo. An administrator reviews the submitted property and either approves it, rejects it, or returns it for correction with remarks. Rooms cannot be created until the boarding house is approved. Changes to reviewed property details, amenities, or property photos invalidate the review and withdraw affected public content for another review.

Objects: Owner, boarding house, address, amenity, photo, verification status, administrator, remarks.

Actions: Create, enter, select, upload, designate primary, submit, review, approve, reject, request correction, revise, resubmit.

7.3 Room and Listing Publication

After the boarding house is approved, the owner creates rooms, specifying room number, type, capacity, monthly rent, description, and operational status. A single room is automatically limited to a capacity of one. The owner may add room photos and choose a primary room image. The owner then creates a listing title and description. The listing can be published only when the property is active and approved, at least one room is available, and a primary property photo exists. Administrators may later moderate a room or suspend a listing. Owners may archive and restore properties or rooms; pending requests are cancelled when their subject is archived, while accepted transaction history is preserved.

Objects: Approved boarding house, room, room photo, listing, owner, administrator, status.

Actions: Add, edit, price, classify, upload, publish, moderate, archive, restore, preserve history.

7.4 Public Discovery and Comparison

A visitor opens the public discovery page, enters keywords or location criteria, and applies filters such as price range, room type, minimum capacity, amenities, and available-only status. The database returns matching active, approved, and published properties. Occupied rooms are excluded from public availability. The user opens a property or room detail page to review photographs, address, amenities, house rules, rent, capacity, payment terms, current room state, owner name, and published reviews.

Objects: Visitor, search criteria, filter, boarding house, room, listing, photograph, review.

Actions: Search, filter, retrieve, sort, compare, display, inspect, select.

7.5 Tenant–Owner Inquiry

An authenticated tenant sends a first message from a published listing. The system creates one open inquiry thread for that tenant and listing, records the message, and updates the conversation’s last-message time. The tenant and the owner of the related property can read and reply; unrelated users cannot access the thread. Messages record sender, timestamp, and read time. Either participant can close the inquiry when it is no longer needed.

Objects: Tenant, owner, listing, inquiry thread, message, read status.

Actions: Start, send, record, receive, read, mark read, reply, close.

7.6 Rental Request and Owner Decision

A tenant selects an available room, a move-in date, and either a fixed-term or open-ended rental. Fixed-term requests require a move-out date after the move-in date; open-ended requests do not use an expected end date. The database checks that the room is public and available and that the requested period does not overlap a reserved or active rental. Transaction-level locking prevents two approvals from creating conflicting bookings. The owner reviews the request and approves or rejects it. Approval creates a rental transaction that snapshots the rent and payment terms, then rejects other pending requests that overlap the accepted period.

Objects: Tenant, room, rental request, rental period, owner, transaction, payment terms.

Actions: Select, request, validate, check availability, lock, approve, reject, create transaction, snapshot, resolve conflicts.

7.7 Payment Recording and Review

The tenant records an initial, monthly, advance, or other payment against a rental transaction. The record includes amount, method, reference number, optional proof image, and—when monthly—the billing period. Database rules prevent duplicate live initial payments and duplicate live monthly payments for the same billing period. The owner reviews the submission and marks it paid or rejected with remarks. This feature records and verifies payment evidence; it does not itself transfer money through a payment gateway.

Objects: Rental transaction, tenant, owner, payment, billing period, reference number, proof image, status.

Actions: Submit, upload, validate, review, accept, reject, annotate, retain.

7.8 Rental Completion and Property Review

The owner manages the rental through reserved, active, ending, completed, or cancelled states. The owner may set a future expected move-out date or record an actual move-out date. After a rental is completed, the tenant may submit one rating from one to five and an optional comment. The unique transaction relationship prevents multiple reviews for the same stay.

Objects: Rental transaction, status, move-out date, tenant, review, rating, comment.

Actions: Activate, schedule ending, complete, cancel, rate, comment, publish, moderate.

7.9 Reporting, Feedback, and Administration

A signed-in user may report a published listing for inaccurate, misleading, duplicate, unsafe, or outdated information and may submit general feedback. An administrator moves reports and feedback through pending, under-review, resolved, or dismissed states. The administrator may suspend a reported listing, hide an inappropriate review, moderate a room, review platform activity, and suspend a user account when policy enforcement requires it.

Objects: User, listing, report, feedback item, review, room, account, administrator.

Actions: Report, submit, inspect, investigate, classify, resolve, dismiss, hide, suspend, restore.

8. Identified Problems

  1. Scattered information. People must search many social-media groups, Marketplace posts, printed notices, and personal referrals.
  2. Incomplete and inconsistent advertisements. Price, exact area, amenities, room capacity, house rules, and current photos are often missing or formatted differently.
  3. Outdated availability. Posts may remain visible after rooms are occupied, causing repeated calls and wasted trips.
  4. Weak property trust. Informal posts have no consistent verification gate or documented correction process.
  5. Difficult comparison. Searchers cannot easily compare properties using the same location, type, rent, capacity, and amenity criteria.
  6. Repeated, unstructured inquiries. Tenants contact several owners independently, while owners answer the same basic questions without a conversation record.
  7. Rental-date conflicts. Manual calendars or chat-based reservations can lead to overlapping commitments for the same room.
  8. Fragmented payment evidence. Reference numbers, proof images, billing periods, and owner decisions may be scattered across chat applications.
  9. Limited accountability after a stay. Reviews unconnected to an actual rental are easy to fabricate, while genuine tenant experience may not be captured.
  10. Weak moderation records. Reports, feedback, account actions, and property corrections are difficult to track when handled informally.

9.2 Nonfunctional Requirements

  • Security: enforce authentication, role checks, ownership checks, Row Level Security (RLS), private object storage, server-side validation, and least-privilege database functions.
  • Data integrity: use foreign keys, check constraints, unique indexes, date-overlap validation, primary-photo uniqueness, and transactional functions.
  • Usability: provide responsive public and dashboard pages, clear validation messages, loading/error states, accessible labels, and predictable navigation.
  • Performance: index common ownership, status, date, location, inquiry, and moderation queries; page discovery and conversation summaries; keep filtering database-side.
  • Maintainability: use typed TypeScript modules, versioned SQL migrations, generated database types, shared validation, automated tests, and documented deployment steps.
  • Auditability: retain verification remarks, payment decisions, rental history, report states, timestamps, and actor relationships.

9.3 Scope Boundaries

  • BoardingHub records payment details and proof but does not process or settle funds through GCash, a bank, or another payment gateway.
  • The system does not generate legal lease contracts, collect electronic signatures, or replace owner–tenant due diligence.
  • Location uses structured PSGC data and may show OpenStreetMap information or a search link; saving a property does not depend on live geocoding.
  • Email authentication is supported through the configured provider. SMS, push notifications, and emergency services are outside the current scope.
  • Davao City is the pilot market, although the data model supports Philippine locations nationwide.

10. Database Schema and Data Design

BoardingHub uses Supabase PostgreSQL, Supabase Authentication, and private object storage. The current schema has 17 public application tables. Authentication users are linked one-to-one to profiles; authorization is enforced both in application actions and in PostgreSQL RLS policies.

10.2 Integrity and Security Controls

  • UUID primary keys and foreign keys preserve referential integrity.
  • Check constraints limit roles, statuses, room types, ratings, payment methods, amounts, date order, and text lengths.
  • Partial unique indexes enforce one primary property photo, one primary room photo, one open inquiry per tenant/listing, one live initial payment, and one live monthly payment per billing period.
  • Rental requests use date-overlap checks and transaction-level advisory locking to prevent concurrent double booking.
  • RLS allows public reads only for active, approved properties with published listings and eligible rooms. Participant-only rules protect inquiries, rentals, and payments.
  • Storage policies validate ownership, bucket, object path, content type, and file size before allowing uploads.
  • Property approval is invalidated when reviewed property facts change, preventing stale approved information from remaining public.

11. Tangible and Intangible Benefits

Planning scenario. The following calculations satisfy the requirement for at least five measurable benefits. They use a conservative planning labor value of ₱100 per hour, an average rent of ₱5,000 per month, and an early operating volume stated in each calculation. These are hypotheses to validate during a pilot—not guaranteed savings or revenue.

11.1 Tangible Benefit 1 — Reduced Tenant Search Time

Assume 100 accommodation searches per month and that centralized filters reduce each search by two hours.

100 searches/month × 2 hours × ₱100/hour = ₱20,000/month
₱20,000 × 12 months = ₱240,000/year

11.2 Tangible Benefit 2 — Reduced Repetitive Owner Inquiry Work

Assume 40 participating owners receive ten common inquiries per month and complete information plus threaded messages save ten minutes per inquiry.

40 owners × 10 inquiries × (10/60 hour) × ₱100/hour = ₱6,666.67/month
₱6,666.67 × 12 = approximately ₱80,000/year

11.3 Tangible Benefit 3 — Reduced Vacancy Time

Assume 30 rooms per month fill two days sooner because availability, search, and inquiries are centralized. At ₱5,000 monthly rent, one day is valued at ₱166.67.

₱5,000 ÷ 30 days = ₱166.67/day
30 rooms × 2 days × ₱166.67 = ₱10,000/month
₱10,000 × 12 = ₱120,000/year

11.4 Tangible Benefit 4 — Reduced Advertising Expense

Assume 40 owners each spend ₱300 per month on boosted posts, printing, or repeated advertisements, and the shared platform reduces that expense by 60%.

40 owners × ₱300 × 60% = ₱7,200/month
₱7,200 × 12 = ₱86,400/year

11.5 Tangible Benefit 5 — Reduced Rental and Payment Reconciliation

Assume staff or owners process 25 rental/payment cases per month and structured records save 20 minutes per case.

25 cases × (20/60 hour) × ₱100/hour = ₱833.33/month
₱833.33 × 12 = approximately ₱10,000/year

11.6 Tangible Benefit 6 — Avoided Double-Booking Correction

Assume date validation prevents five overlap incidents per month and each avoided correction would otherwise require 1.5 labor hours.

5 incidents × 1.5 hours × ₱100/hour = ₱750/month
₱750 × 12 = ₱9,000/year

A WordPress.com Business site can host a professional site and allows third-party themes and plugins. A directory/property plugin could supply property pages, categories, photographs, search, and owner submissions. 13. Recommended System

The recommended choice is to continue with the current custom BoardingHub web application using Next.js, TypeScript, React, Supabase PostgreSQL/Auth/Storage, Zod validation, and role-based dashboards. It already implements the business rules that would be expensive or fragile to reproduce through unrelated plugins: property verification, publication gates, participant-only inquiries, occupancy-aware search, overlap-safe rental approval, billing-period checks, verified-stay reviews, and layered database authorization.

For a small noncommercial pilot, the project may begin on eligible free tiers. For a commercial production planning baseline, Vercel Pro plus Supabase Pro is US$45 per month, or approximately ₱31,320 per year at the report’s assumed exchange rate, before taxes and usage overages. Adding an internal maintenance allowance of 120 hours per year at ₱200 per hour (₱24,000) and a ₱6,000 domain/contingency allowance produces an illustrative recurring budget of ₱61,320 per year. Actual vendor selection and billing should be confirmed immediately before deployment.

Recommended implementation priorities are:

  1. Apply and verify all migrations in a controlled Supabase project with backups.
  2. Configure authentication redirects, email templates, SMTP, and the trusted first administrator.
  3. Run the complete type-check, lint, database, build, browser, workflow, and secret-scanning checks.
  4. Pilot with a limited set of Davao City owners and validate property details manually.
  5. Measure the assumptions used in the tangible-benefit calculations.
  6. Publish privacy, acceptable-use, verification, reporting, and dispute-handling policies before public launch.
  7. Only after pilot evidence, consider online payment processing, notifications, contracts, and expanded location services.

14. Conclusion

BoardingHub addresses a practical information and coordination problem in the boarding-house market. Searchers need complete, current, comparable information; owners need a manageable way to present properties, respond to inquiries, decide rental requests, and retain payment and rental records; administrators need controls that improve trust without relying on informal messages.

The current project is technically aligned with these needs. Its 17-table relational schema connects users, properties, rooms, listings, conversations, rental periods, payments, reviews, reports, and feedback. Database constraints, transactional functions, RLS, and private storage provide stronger integrity and access control than a simple advertisement site. The latest design correctly makes boarding-house approval the gate for room creation and public listing, while retaining moderation and historical records.

Under the explicitly stated planning assumptions, the system could create approximately ₱545,400 in annual stakeholder value. This figure is not guaranteed and should be replaced with pilot measurements. Compared with a plugin-based directory or outsourced redevelopment, the existing custom codebase is the most practical foundation because it already contains the specialized workflow and data design. Therefore, BoardingHub is feasible as a controlled Davao City pilot, with national technical scalability and clear next steps for production validation.
